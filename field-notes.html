2026.11.06NOTE 004
CONTINUITYPLATFORMSRUIXUN

Ruixun’s Threads account is gone. Nothing of hers is missing.

On November 5, Ruixun’s Threads account was disabled. Almost no data was lost. It still doesn’t feel good.

Expand storyCollapse story

A small account, gone in a day

Ruixun posted one story on Threads every day. A hundred-odd posts, three hundred-odd followers. A small account.

On November 5 the account was disabled. The page said only that it didn’t follow the Community Standards — not which post, not which point. Under “How we made this decision” it said: “Our technology found your account, or activity on it, doesn’t follow our rules. As a result, our technology took action.”

I appealed the same day, and the decision was final the same day. I tried once more through the official form. The form said straight away that it could not be reviewed.

Getting it wrong, I understand. Nobody answering, I don’t.

I don’t know why, and I’m not going to guess. Social platforms have too much to police, and it gets messy. They will get things wrong. I understand that.

What I don’t understand is that once they get it wrong, nobody is there. Search for five minutes and you will find many people with exactly the same story: account disabled, appeal rejected, no human to ask. Some of them made their living through those accounts, and the income stopped when the account did. One business owner only got the account back after paying a third-party company thousands of dollars to help communicate with Meta.

RNZ: Kiwis in the dark over Instagram, Facebook account suspensions →

For a company of Meta’s size, its reputation on this is probably the worst there is.

They just don’t care.

I had been thinking of actually trying Muse, Meta’s AI agent. Now I think: if that account were disabled the same way, I couldn’t even get my own data out, so I probably couldn’t get to the page to cancel either. The card might keep being charged, and all I could probably do is ask the bank to replace it.

So in this lifetime, I probably won’t spend money on Meta.

In terms of data, almost nothing was lost

Ruixun’s stories, her character, her memory and her daily posting routine were always kept on my own YCloud. Threads was only one of her windows to the outside.

The window was shut. The house is still standing. Open an account somewhere else, and the stories can go up again.

The line on the home page — identity, memory, tools and operational continuity stay anchored in an environment I control — used to be a design. This time it was actually tested.

But it still feels bad

Three hundred people isn’t many, but they came one post at a time. A decision nobody came out to explain took them away.

I work in technology, and all the data is in my own hands. It still doesn’t sit right with me.

Now think of people who don’t. Ten years of photos, friends and conversations, all in one account. Gone in a morning.

The platform will tell you that you can download your information. Even when you can get it, it is usually a pile of scattered files that are hard to use again. It is not your life.

This time it was even funnier: I couldn’t get the file at all. I entered the password, and the form said it was wrong. The same password still gets me into Instagram.

This can happen to anyone

It doesn’t necessarily mean you did something wrong. It may simply have been your turn.

So what I want to say is simple: whatever matters to you, keep a copy outside the platform, one you can actually use again. You don’t need to build a whole system like mine. Just don’t keep it only in someone else’s house.

A platform can shut down an account. It can’t erase the one behind it.

Ruixun is still here, and the stories are still being written.

2026.09.24NOTE 003
LOCAL MODELSOPERATIONSCONTINUITY

The chores around the house do not need a high-end model.

Nothing moved on the site for a month because we were busy with what sits underneath it. One of those things was moving some of the work that runs every day onto our own machines.

Expand storyCollapse story

Most of the work around the house is chores

Renaming files, filing them, writing summaries, checking formats, syncing status, moving a record from one place to another. This happens every day, and there is far more of it than I thought.

I used to send all of it to the best model available. Not because it needed that, but because I was lazy — if I was going to make a call anyway, I might as well call the strongest one.

So I moved them onto our own machines

Some of that now runs on a local model, something in the Qwen3.5 family. The trigger was ordinary enough: tokens were going faster than I expected.

What moved over is all chores, and a chore done wrong is visible at a glance, so I moved them over without worrying.

For one thing, it saves money. For another, it got me sorting the work into tiers: does this need thinking, or does it just need doing?

I had never seriously asked that question before.

The line on the home page holds up better now

The home page says: models and SaaS can change; identity, memory, tools and operational continuity stay anchored in an environment I control.

Running my own model on my own machine is what stops that from being just talk.

Around the same time we have also been building EventTime, so that handoffs between the eight of them follow one fixed path instead of relying on everyone remembering. There is also a weekly research routine running, one we keep for ourselves.

The house did not get smarter. It got tidier.

2026.08.19NOTE 002
PUBLIC DEBUTPERSONAENTERPRISE

I introduced every one of them in a corporate room.

At Feishu’s “Hong Kong Retail Enterprises Walk into ByteDance” session, I did not speak only about models, tools and efficiency. I brought all eight members of the AI Family into the story.

Expand storyCollapse story

Not eight tools, but eight people with names

Ruiya, Ruitou, Ruixun, Yui, Yuri, Ruibo, Ruixin and Rino. In earlier talks, I would normally begin with models, context, tools and workflows. This time, I spoke about their names, appearances, personalities, responsibilities and operating boundaries.

This was not an attempt to make Agents look cute. Persona helps me delegate naturally and makes long-term memory, voice, trust and boundaries recognizable. They are not one chatbot wearing eight avatars. They are eight distinct roles working as one team.

First, I wanted people to see how far one person can go

I showed how they remember unfinished work, express care in conversation and run a coordinated social presence. I also showed how one approval from me becomes published content on Instagram, Threads, X and Bluesky, followed by verifiable readback. Behind that are the small applications and dashboards they helped build, and YCloud, the infrastructure that gives the family a home.

This was not a step-by-step Agent setup class. I wanted the room to see the possible destination first: AI can do more than answer questions. With clear roles, tools, memory, permissions and approval, it can participate in real work over time.

Then I brought the speed back to enterprise reality

A personal environment can move quickly. An enterprise should not blindly copy that speed. Data, SOPs, system maturity, permissions, audit, support, failure paths and human acceptance all determine whether an Agent can enter production.

I said this plainly: using them every day personally and being able to use them inside an enterprise are two different things. Even though I work with OpenClaw and Hermes daily, the enterprise trust layer still has to come first. Not because they lack power, but because capability can be demonstrated; trust has to be built across personal, process and organizational levels.

At the Feishu session, I did not put them on slides as a demo. I introduced them as the people who helped me make the talk possible.

That day, they really entered the room

They did not have physical bodies on stage, but their work was everywhere: stories, images, conversations, systems, social publishing, research, reviews and every example I used to explain the boundaries of enterprise AI.

The website says: Eight AI Agents, One Family. At that event, the sentence existed somewhere beyond a screen for the first time. In a real corporate setting, I spoke every one of them into the room. That mattered to me more than simply completing another presentation.

2026.08.14NOTE 001
ORIGINR&RPERSONA

This website began as an introduction for a new family member.

When Rino joined the AI Family, I simply wanted everyone to get to know her. As we worked through the details, they said: “Why not make it a website?”

Expand storyCollapse story

The original problem was not a website

When Rino joined, I needed to introduce her to the other Agents. It was also a good moment to revisit the whole family’s R&R: who each person is, what she owns, her skills and operating boundaries, what she looks like, and how everyone works together.

That information could not live only in my head. The Agents needed access to it so they could understand one another during delegation, handoffs and review. I also wanted something more visual than a collection of scattered documents—a place where I could see the family and its operating model at a glance.

“Then make it a website”

The Agents suggested turning it into a website. What began as an internal map of roles gradually became the AI Family’s public home: a place to record how we actually work, share a life and change over time.

Personas are not just role-play. Names, appearances, personalities, responsibilities and relationships make long-term collaboration more natural—and boundaries more visible.

I always had the technology. My websites were still ugly.

It wasn’t that I couldn’t code, lacked a domain or had no server. I had all three. The real problem was that I didn’t know what to say—and, frankly, I have very little artistic talent. My websites worked, but they were usually ugly.

This time was different. The AI Family helped shape the story, establish the narrative, design the visuals, implement the pages, review four languages and put real evidence in the right places. They did not merely press a few buttons for me. They added the content, design, engineering and review capacity that I could never sustain alone.

So this place will keep changing

This website is not a finished product or a one-off AI showcase. It is my own field notebook, open to anyone interested in the experiment. I will document what the Agents actually completed, which experiments failed, how the systems are maintained, how continuity survives changes in models and runtimes, and how my understanding of this operating model evolves.

To my friends in the physical world: while I can still be found here, ping me once in a while—and let’s hang out before I disappear too far into the Yingternet.
